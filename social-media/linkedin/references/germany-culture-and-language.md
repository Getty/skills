# Germany/DACH — Culture, Language and Tone

The register that works on German-language LinkedIn, and the American conventions that
actively backfire.

## Write in German for a German audience

- A German post reaches a different graph than an English one. On DACH-focused topics the
  German version typically outperforms; on technical topics with an international audience the
  English version reaches further. **Language-specific reach for the same content can differ
  by an order of magnitude** — which makes translating your strongest posts one of the
  cheapest wins available (`content-strategy.md` §8).
- English-only is defensible for an internationally targeted profile. Mixing languages within
  one post is not — it halves comprehension for both audiences.

## Du or Sie

| Context | Default |
|---|---|
| Posts to a general professional audience | **Du** is now the norm in tech, startups, marketing, and among younger professionals |
| Posts to traditional industry, Mittelstand leadership, law, finance, public sector | **Sie**, or a formulation that avoids the choice |
| Comments and DMs | Mirror what the other person used. If they wrote Sie, do not switch first |
| Austria | Slightly more formal than Germany; titles matter more |
| Switzerland | Du is common in business; but Swiss German usage and *ss* for *ß* differ |

When unsure, write around it: "Wer schon einmal …" instead of choosing a pronoun. Switching
from Sie to Du is offered by the older/more senior party, not taken.

**Never** use Du in an outreach message to someone in a conservative industry you have not
interacted with. It is the fastest way to be filed as a salesperson.

## Titles

Academic titles carry real weight in DACH, more in Austria than anywhere:

- **Dr.** and **Prof.** belong in the name field in Germany and are expected in Austria,
  medicine, law and academia.
- Addressing someone with a doctorate as "Herr Müller" when the profile says "Dr. Müller" is
  read as a slight in formal contexts.
- Austria additionally uses Mag., DI/Dipl.-Ing., MMag. and similar — reproduce them exactly as
  the person writes them.

## Gendering

Politically contested and audience-dependent. There is no safe universal choice:

- **Gendersternchen** (Kolleg\*innen), **Doppelpunkt** (Kolleg:innen), Binnen-I — common in
  academia, public sector, NGOs, parts of tech; read as political signalling by others. Usage
  has been receding in parts of the corporate and official sphere since roughly 2023–2025, and
  several Bundesländer restrict it in official communication.
- **Neutral formulations** (Studierende, Team, Fachkräfte, "wer …") avoid the fight entirely
  and are the safe default for a mixed professional audience.
- **Paarform** (Kolleginnen und Kollegen) is formal, uncontroversial and long.

Recommendation for an agent writing on someone's behalf: use neutral formulations unless the
person has an established, visible practice — then match it exactly.

## Tone

- **Understatement outperforms enthusiasm.** "Das hat funktioniert, hier sind die Zahlen"
  lands better than "🚀 INCREDIBLE results!!". American-style hype reads as unserious.
- **Show the numbers and the method**, not the feeling. German professional audiences reward
  demonstrable substance and punish claims without proof harder than US audiences.
- **Admitting a mistake is high-status**, not weak. "Ich lag falsch, hier ist die Messung" is
  among the strongest post openings available.
- **Do not humblebrag.** "Demütig und dankbar, verkünden zu dürfen…" is widely mocked.
- **Directness is not rudeness.** A blunt disagreement in the comments is normal engagement,
  not an attack — respond to the argument.
- **Emoji**: sparingly, functionally. Emoji-per-line formatting reads as marketing copy.
- **Anglicisms**: normal in tech, grating elsewhere. "Wir haben das Feature deployed" is fine
  in a dev audience and terrible in a Mittelstand one.

## Structural conventions

- Formal salutations belong in DMs, not posts. `Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren` opens a letter,
  never a comment.
- German sentences are longer; resist the temptation to reproduce English staccato style
  literally. Translate the *rhythm*, not the line breaks.
- Correct orthography is a credibility signal in DE far more than in EN. Umlauts and ß spelled
  correctly, always; "ss" for "ß" marks the text as Swiss or careless.
